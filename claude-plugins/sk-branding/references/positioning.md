# Positioning: where we are, where we could be, where we should not be

## 1. What a brand is (The Futur, Chris Do)
- Not a logo (that is a symbol), not the product, not a promise, not an impression. It is the result: the gut feeling a person has about a product, service or company. It lives in their head and heart, so every person holds a slightly different version.
- A brand is reputation. Everyone in the company builds it with every interaction.
- Goal: one coherent identity instead of a war between conflicting versions of yourself.
- A logo alone changes nothing without strategy. A full identity system is logo, typographic recipes, colors and patterns, all serving positioning.

## 2. Specialize (The Futur, "Why being good at everything leads nowhere")
- A generalist loses to the specialist. A broad portfolio signals shallow process knowledge; the specialist asks the deeper industry questions and wins the deal.
- Build skills like a tree trunk: one strong trunk (T-shaped) first. A second deep trunk (Pi-shaped) comes later, often around 40; many trunks (comb-shaped) after decades. Build them one after another, so the first pays for the next.
- Ladder of recognition: unknown → expert (deliberate practice, awards, judging) → authority (top 10 in the niche; other experts name you) → influencer (known outside your industry). Climb through content and reputation. Influence without expertise is hollow.
- Sell the business or emotional value, not the craft. A client does not want a vector file; they want to be positioned, stand out and communicate.

Niche test: can the user finish "I help <specific client> get <specific result> without <main objection>"? If not, keep grilling.

## 3. Positioning method (external: April Dunford, "Obviously Awesome")
Work in this order:
1. Competitive alternatives: what clients would do if you did not exist (including DIY, an employee, doing nothing).
2. Unique attributes: what you have that the alternatives lack (capabilities, method, proof, experience).
3. Value: what those attributes enable for the client, with proof.
4. Best-fit clients: who cares most about that value, and the traits that make them care.
5. Market category: the frame that makes your value obvious. Options: an existing category (win head-on), a subsegment of one (win a niche), or a new category (expensive; only with strong proof and budget).
6. Then trends that make it relevant now (use with care).

## 4. Onlyness statement (external: Marty Neumeier, "Zag")
"Our brand is the only <category> that <differentiator> for <client> in <place> who <need> during <era>."
If it is hard to write, the brand is not different yet. If many brands could sign it, it is not true.
Zag rule: when everybody zigs, zag. Study the herd and do the opposite (also Sabri Suby).

## 5. Enemy and stance
- True fans need a declared enemy. Name it and you own the conversation. Examples: Dr. Squatch vs "Big Soap", Dove vs unrealistic beauty standards, The Futur vs crippling art-school debt.
- Template: "We are in business to put an end to: <problem, practice or belief>."
- The enemy is a problem, practice or belief, never a person. One complaint is fine; the same complaint twice makes you a whiner. Turn the frustration into a mission and a solution.
- Brand as a movement (the Apple pattern): a shared belief and an "us vs them" make a tribe. Use it honestly: the tribe is united by a value, not by fear or contempt.

## 6. Two strategy schools, and when each applies
- Niche and differentiation (Chris Do, Dunford, Neumeier): best for personal brands, consultants, agencies and small service firms with limited budget. Focus wins.
- Mental and physical availability (external: Byron Sharp, "How Brands Grow"): best for consumer products with many buyers. Growth comes from reaching many light buyers, being easy to think of in buying situations (category entry points) and easy to buy, and using consistent distinctive brand assets (color, shape, character, sound, slogan) for years.
- Reconcile: narrow offer and position, broad and accessible content (Brendan Kane's generalist principle), and consistent distinctive assets in both cases.

## 7. Where we should not be (checks)
- Competing on price against cheaper players. A price-led position is a race to the bottom unless the cost structure truly wins.
- Clients outside the best-fit profile, even when they pay. They bring wrong-fit referrals.
- Services kept "because clients ask" that dilute the specialist position.
- Me-too claims: quality, great service, experienced team, innovative, responsive, trusted for 30 years. Every competitor says these. They are not a position.
- A new category without proof and budget to educate the market.
- Many channels at once before one channel is profitable.
- Hype markets with low barriers that saturate fast (example: generic AI service agencies). Saturation comes quickly; the hard part is selling, not producing.
- Copying trends instead of mastering formats.

## 8. Market opportunity lenses ("where we could be")
- Adjacent move: same client, next problem (value ladder up) or same skill, a better-paying client.
- Bolt-on technology for traditional businesses: example, AI receptionists that answer missed calls for plumbers, dentists and cleaners. Pattern: find a stable industry's costly bottleneck and sell the fix, not the tool.
- Productize a service (external: John Warrillow, "Built to Sell"): a fixed scope, fixed price package that a team can sell and deliver.
- Authority path: expert → authority → influencer via content and partnerships.
- AI search: check how assistants answer "best <category> for <client>". A clear niche, consistent naming across sites and third-party mentions raise the chance to be cited.
Evaluate each option: fit with strengths, size of the client's problem (big problem = big budget), competition, proof needed, time to first revenue.
