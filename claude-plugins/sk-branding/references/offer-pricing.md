# Offer, pricing and the sales conversation

Premise (Sabri Suby): the market does not pay for the best product; it pays for solving problems. The bigger the problem, the bigger the pay. A compelling offer beats a convincing argument.

## 1. Features to benefits
Two columns: every feature you can think of, then the benefit each gives the client. "Features tell, benefits sell." Done well, offers almost write themselves.

## 2. Weak offers (never the core of an offer)
Great service, outstanding quality, innovative, great team, responsive, great reputation. A vague "free consultation". A guarantee of "satisfaction".

## 3. Godfather offer: seven parts (Sabri Suby)
Test: if the offer and guarantee do not keep the founder up at night, they are not strong enough.
1. Rationale: a believable reason why the offer is so generous.
2. Build value: anchor on the normal or competitor price; show where it sold at full price; put a money value on the result and compare.
3. Pricing: for cold traffic, a low-risk front offer; then 1-2 higher tiers or upsells.
4. Payment options: split high prices into 3-4 payments.
5. Premiums: bonuses that come with the purchase.
6. Power guarantee: move the risk from the buyer to you.
7. Scarcity: only real limits (deadline, capacity, stock, price rise). Fake scarcity erodes trust.
Then: list the main objections, answer each, and ask "what can I add to make this even more compelling?"
For services: the front offer is often a specific free session, analysis or roadmap with a named outcome, not a generic "free consultation".

## 4. Power guarantee (7 steps)
1. Study competitor guarantees. 2. Build on your strength (speed, a specific result). 3. Guarantee a concrete outcome. 4. Choose a payback that costs you little and looks valuable. 5. Test. 6. Show it everywhere, in writing. 7. Name it ("Iron-Clad …", "Better than Risk Free"). Most buyers never claim it; honest ones say yes more easily.

## 5. Value equation (external: Alex Hormozi, "$100M Offers")
Value = (dream outcome × perceived likelihood of success) / (time delay × effort and sacrifice). Raise the top, cut the bottom. Use it to compare offer variants.

## 6. Offer stack: Hook + TBIF (8-Stage Offer Plan)
- Hook: from the before and after grid (agitate pain or show transformation).
- Function, Brand, Influence, Transaction attributes (see `ideal-client.md` section 6).
- Mix and match; not all five are needed. Test 3 stacks (ads first, then apply winners to pages and emails).
- Example stack: Hook "Get your kids off Snapchat" / Function "2022 boards with new fiberglass tech" / Brand "We started as dads on a mission to have more fun with our kids" / Influence "My kids want to be on the lake all summer!" / Transaction "Buy a board, get a free vest".

## 7. Offer harmonics and lifetime value
- Track CAC (cost to acquire a client), AOV (average order value), CLV (customer lifetime value). If you cannot pay to acquire a client, you do not have a business yet. Know what a client is worth, and you know what you can spend to win one.
- Do not chase only new clients. Give the right offer at each stage of the journey: first purchase, waiting for delivery (status updates and usage tips keep excitement high), unboxing, review request (personal, as if from the CEO), repeat purchase (points, accessories), fan (referrals, social posts). Rewards grow with loyalty.
- Value ladder: entry → core → premium → ongoing. Upgrade paths must be justified by real value (the Apple ladder works because each step adds something).
- Unboxing and first experience are part of the brand (Apple's packaging as a "dopamine box"). Small touches: a thank-you card, a small extra gift, birthday and seasonal emails.

## 8. Pricing stance (The Futur)
- Value-based pricing: tie the fee to the business result. Script: Why change now, rather than leave it? If solved, how many extra clients a month? What is one client worth over time? So it is worth <clients × LTV × 12> a year. What share of that is a fair investment? Fee as 10-15% of value, or hybrid: small upfront fee plus revenue share.
- For teams selling without the founder, value pricing is hard. Use fixed packages with public scope, timeline and price (productized service). The client knows the price before the call.
- Do not justify prices. Explaining your margin concedes the higher ground. Negotiate scope, never the rate: split must-haves (MVP) from nice-to-haves; offer the MVP plus one add-on and recommend the highest-ROI option. If the client wants a lower price for the same scope: "This is the price. For every client there is a vendor; maybe I am not the right one for you." A client who demands justification at once is often a difficult client later.
- Anchor first and high. The first number sets perceived value. If the client lowballs: ignore it, wait, then re-anchor ("I can help you find someone for 10; if you want me, it is 30"), or say "That's not going to work. Can you come up with another number?"
- Low budget vs big problem (Jim Rohn: do not make a minor effort for a major goal): ask what it costs if the problem is not solved. A small budget for a million-sized problem is a logic error; position your fee as cheap insurance against that loss. Refer them to cheaper providers if they cannot pay.
- Budget follows the perceived size of the problem. Small problem, small budget.
- Each price you quote is the lowest the client will ever hear from you; experience raises it.
- Price fewer, better projects higher. Protect margin and keep a cash reserve (about a year of salaries) instead of chasing revenue.
- Scope crisis script: offer three choices: finish at a loss but no more work at this rate; add budget and continue; or end now with all files handed over.

## 9. The sales conversation (for website CTAs and offer design)
- Selling is change management: from the current state, past an obstacle, to the desired state. The client always has three options: you, someone else, nothing.
- Inbound leads already see you as qualified. The call can only build or break that trust.
- Ask, do not pitch. Emotion decides, facts justify. Let the client argue for the change.
- 3x Why (Chris Do): Why this? Why now? Why us?
- Diagnose before you prescribe ("A prescription without a diagnosis is malpractice", Suby): motive → 12-month future → admission that past attempts failed → a small win on the call → "Does this sound like the help you need?" → tailored offer → close.
- Prepare answers to the top 5 objections: experience, industry knowledge, price, timing, team size.
- Price-shopper opener: "I will probably be the highest bid. Is your decision based mainly on price?"
- Pain opener: "Clients like you usually tell me their top three problems are X, Y and Z. Is that right?"
- Conditional close: "So what I heard is <their words>. If you saw a solution that did this, this and that, in this time, for this money, would you move forward?" Wait for an emphatic yes. Send the proposal the same day; follow up the next working day.
- State the price with conviction, then stay silent. A fast-action bonus (e.g. waived setup fee if decided on the call or within 24 hours) is fine when real.
- "With or without you" energy: no desperation; refer bad fits elsewhere. Soften hard questions with "Out of pure curiosity, …" and a smile in your voice (Chris Voss). "If it were up to me, I'd hire you" means hidden decision makers: ask how decisions are made.
- Do not sell to bad-fit clients, even if they want to buy.
